An Australian Census Story  /  1911–2021

We grew.We moved.We changed.

Every few years, the Census counts every person in Australia on a single night. Line up 110 years of those snapshots and a larger story appears: how many of us there are, where we live, where we come from, how old we are and who we share a home with.

What is the Census?

A count of every person and household in Australia on Census night, run by the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS). It has been held every five years since 1961.

How to read the charts

Hover over or tap any mark to see exact figures. Buttons above some charts switch the view. Each chart's source and specification file are linked below it.

A note on comparisons

Census methods have changed since 1911. Where a change affects what you see, such as who was counted, a note beside the chart explains it.

Chapter 01 / The scale of change

From a small nation to 25 million lives.

The first national Census in 1911 counted 4.46 million people. The 2021 Census counted 25.4 million, about 5.7 times as many. Growth was not steady, though. It slowed during depression and war, then surged in the decades after 1947.

+3.9mpeople added in a single decade, from the 2011 Census to the 2021 Census.
12.8mpeople counted in 1971, almost exactly half the 2021 total. The population roughly doubled in 50 years.
2.02mpeople added between 2016 and 2021, more than in any other gap between Censuses.

The national total hides a very uneven map. Where, exactly, do all those Australians live, and where did the newest arrivals settle?

Chapter 02 / The geography of growth

One country. Very different places.

In 2021, New South Wales and Victoria together held 57.3% of everyone counted. The largest states by area are among the emptiest. Between 2016 and 2021, the biggest states added the most people, but the fastest growth was in the smallest jurisdiction, the Australian Capital Territory.

Reading the maps: all three maps show whole states and territories. People are not spread evenly within a state; most live in the capital cities and along the coast. Boundaries come from the ABS Australian Statistical Geography Standard (2021) and are drawn with an equal-area projection, so the size of each state on screen matches its real land area. The ABS also counts a small number of people in “Other Territories” (Jervis Bay, Christmas Island, Cocos (Keeling) Islands and Norfolk Island): 4,779 in 2021. They are not shown.

A bigger, more spread-out nation is also a different nation. Who are we now?

Chapter 03 / Who we are

Older, and connected to more of the world.

Two long-running changes shape the modern population. Australians are older: the share aged 65 and over rose from 14.0% to 17.2% in just ten years. And many more Australians were born overseas, with India now the second-largest overseas country of birth. These changes happened at the same time; the charts do not show that one caused the other.

+47.9%

India-born residents grew by 217,967 people in five years, the largest increase of the five, overtaking both New Zealand and China. Together, the top five overseas birthplaces account for 2.97 million people, or 11.7% of everyone counted.

“

More people than ever, but fewer of them under each roof. The story ends at the front door.

Chapter 04 / How we live

The shrinking Australian household.

A growing population did not mean bigger homes. In 1911 the average household held about 4.5 people; by 2021 it held 2.5. Living alone has become common. More than one in four households now has just one person in it.

What sits behind smaller households? The data here show what changed, not why. An ageing population (chapter 3) and more people living alone fit together: AIFS notes that half of women who live alone are 65 or older. Smaller families, later partnering and more housing choices are also part of the picture, but they are not measured in these charts.
